A PAN, LAN, MAN or WAN describes the scope of a computer network. These are useful labels for understanding how devices communicate in a personal space, a building, a city or across distant locations. They are not fixed measurements of speed, security or cost, and a single device can belong to more than one type of network at the same time.

This guide explains each type with real examples, shows how LAN differs from WAN, and clears up a common source of confusion: Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Ethernet and the internet describe different aspects of networking.

How computer networks are classified

One common classification describes the geographical or organizational scope of a network. PAN means Personal Area Network; LAN means Local Area Network; MAN means Metropolitan Area Network; and WAN means Wide Area Network. The categories overlap in practice because geography alone does not describe who owns the devices, how they are connected or how traffic is secured.

For example, a university can operate several building LANs and connect them through a campus backbone. The institution might also use leased connections to remote branches. The first description concerns local networks; the second describes a broader WAN arrangement.

What is a PAN (Personal Area Network)?

A PAN connects devices close to an individual. A smartphone paired with Bluetooth headphones or a smartwatch is a familiar example. A PAN is typically short range, but there is no single maximum distance that applies to every personal-area technology.

PAN example: your phone and accessories

You can connect headphones to a phone over Bluetooth while the phone uses Wi-Fi for internet access. The Bluetooth relationship is personal-area connectivity. The Wi-Fi connection can be part of a LAN. The same phone therefore participates in different networking arrangements without changing its physical location.

A PAN can use technologies other than Bluetooth. The right description depends on what devices exchange data and how, rather than assuming that every device owned by one person is always on the same PAN.

Security considerations for PAN devices

Use trusted pairing prompts, apply firmware updates when supported, and remove forgotten or unrecognized paired devices. Being close to you does not automatically make a wireless accessory secure. Radio capabilities, authentication methods and device settings matter.

What is a LAN (Local Area Network)?

A LAN connects devices in a limited area, such as a home, school, laboratory, office floor or building. It can include Ethernet cables, switches, wireless access points, laptops, phones, printers and servers.

LAN example: a home router and its devices

A laptop connected by Ethernet and a phone connected to the same home Wi-Fi may both participate in the local network. They can often reach a local printer or shared storage without sending that traffic through the public internet, although router settings and access restrictions may prevent direct communication.

Many home routers combine several jobs in one box: an Ethernet switch, a wireless access point, a router between the local network and an internet provider, and often a firewall and DHCP service. This all-in-one design does not make LAN and internet synonyms.

Does every LAN have just one subnet?

No. A company can use virtual LANs (VLANs) and multiple IP subnets inside a single building. Segmentation can separate guest devices from employee computers or isolate sensitive systems. Whether two devices can communicate depends on routing and access-control policies, not only on whether they are physically close.

What is a MAN (Metropolitan Area Network)?

A MAN interconnects networks across a metropolitan region or similar urban area. One example is a service provider connecting several office buildings across a city through fiber infrastructure. The term describes the scale of the network, not a specific protocol that automatically makes it fast or encrypted.

MAN example: multiple locations within a city

Consider a university with buildings in different parts of the same city. It may obtain a metropolitan connection from a network operator, creating links among site LANs. The arrangement may be called a MAN when discussed at city scale. In another business context, the exact same connectivity might be described as part of the organization's WAN.

That overlap is normal. Network names are a simplified way to discuss scope, not rigid categories measured against universal distance thresholds.

What is a WAN (Wide Area Network)?

A WAN connects users, sites or smaller networks across a large geographical area. A company with branches in different cities may use provider services, encrypted tunnels or SD-WAN to connect them. The WAN helps carry traffic between networks that are not in one local site.

WAN example: a company with several branches

Imagine a business with an office LAN in Casablanca, another LAN in Rabat and a third in Marrakech. A WAN can link the sites so authorized staff can access shared services. The company still needs access controls and secure transport; wide-area connectivity alone does not encrypt or authorize every connection.

A WAN can be built using different underlying services, including fiber connections, mobile networks or other operator-provided circuits. Actual latency and throughput depend on link characteristics, distance, congestion and routing.

The difference between PAN,LAN,MAN and WAN

LAN vs WAN: the most important differences

The practical distinction is that a LAN primarily supports communication within a limited area, while a WAN interconnects networks or users across more distant locations. Performance, ownership and security arrangements often differ, but none of those properties alone defines a LAN or WAN.

Is Wi-Fi another type alongside PAN, LAN, MAN and WAN?

Wi-Fi describes wireless networking technology, not the same type of geographical classification. A home wireless network is commonly a WLAN (Wireless Local Area Network). Bluetooth is often associated with personal-area connectivity, whereas Ethernet is common in LANs. The internet can connect networks at worldwide scale.

A wired LAN can have no Wi-Fi at all. Likewise, a Wi-Fi signal does not guarantee internet access. A device can show that it is connected to the wireless access point even while the provider connection is down or access is blocked.

Are the internet, intranet and extranet also network-size types?

Not exactly. The internet is a global interconnection of networks. An intranet is a private organizational environment restricted to authorized users. An extranet provides selected outsiders with access to designated organizational resources. These names describe reachability and access purpose, not the same geographic-scale classification as LAN or WAN.

A company may host intranet services on a LAN and also make those services accessible to remote employees through a secure WAN connection. What determines access is the security architecture and policy.

Do all four network types use the same IP addresses?

Not necessarily. A simple Bluetooth PAN might not use IP in the way a typical Ethernet LAN does. A home LAN often uses private IPv4 addresses; routers connect those networks through the provider to services on the internet. A MAN or WAN can carry different link and network protocols depending on the service.

It helps to separate network size from the IP addressing scheme. IP subnets, VLANs, routing and network address translation are additional concepts that can appear across different architectures.

How do these labels relate to cybersecurity?

None of the four types guarantees security. On a PAN, pairing and device updates are important. On a LAN, separate trusted and guest devices, patch network equipment and restrict administration. On metropolitan or wide-area links, assess vendor responsibilities, encryption, authentication, route exposure and monitoring requirements.

A useful rule is to ask who can reach the network and which paths are trusted. An office LAN with an exposed management interface may be less safe than a carefully managed remote connection; the geographical label cannot answer that question.

How to identify the network type in an exercise

  1. List the devices that need to communicate.
  2. Describe the physical locations: next to one person, inside one site, across a city, or across several distant sites.
  3. Identify the technology used, but do not confuse it with network scope.
  4. Explain which smaller networks are interconnected.
  5. State that the labels are approximate when a scenario spans more than one category.

Exercise 1: a phone and Bluetooth headset form a typical PAN. Exercise 2: twenty PCs connected to a classroom switch form a LAN. Exercise 3: three offices in one metropolitan region can be linked by a MAN service. Exercise 4: company branches in two countries can communicate over a WAN.
