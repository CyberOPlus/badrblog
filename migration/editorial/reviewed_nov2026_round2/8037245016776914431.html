When several devices share a transmission medium, they need rules for deciding when to send data. CSMA/CD, CSMA/CA and token passing are different media-access methods developed for different network technologies. They are often compared in networking courses, but they are not three interchangeable options on a modern Ethernet or Wi-Fi router.

The most important distinction is this: CSMA/CD is associated with classic shared, half-duplex Ethernet; CSMA/CA is associated with wireless LAN contention; and token passing grants transmission opportunities through possession of a token. Modern full-duplex switched Ethernet does not need collision detection on a shared bus.

Comparing CSMA CD, CSMA CA and token passing

Why do networks need a medium-access method?

Imagine several computers connected to a common physical cable or several wireless devices trying to use the same radio channel. If their transmissions interfere, receivers may not decode a frame successfully. A media access control (MAC) method establishes how stations coordinate attempts to send, defer when the medium is busy, react to failed transmissions or receive permission to transmit.

Not every network has a shared collision domain. On a point-to-point full-duplex Ethernet link, each end can transmit and receive simultaneously on dedicated paths; a legacy collision-detection procedure is unnecessary. On Wi-Fi, the channel is shared and stations cannot always reliably detect one another.

CSMA/CD: Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection

In CSMA/CD, a station first listens for activity on a shared Ethernet medium. If the medium appears idle, the station transmits. When overlapping transmissions cause a collision, the station detects the conflict, stops the affected transmission and uses a randomized backoff before trying again. A sender cannot simply assume that waiting once means nobody else will transmit.

How a collision and retry work

  1. Station A senses the shared medium and begins sending a frame.
  2. Station B, at another point on the same medium, may also sense an idle channel before A's signal arrives.
  3. The overlapping signals cause a collision on the shared segment.
  4. The stations apply collision handling and backoff, then retry later.

This explains why collision detection was meaningful on historical coaxial or hub-based Ethernet. It also explains why propagation delay and cable limits mattered in classic Ethernet design.

Does a normal Ethernet switch still use CSMA/CD?

Ordinary switched Ethernet ports configured for full-duplex operation do not use CSMA/CD. The Ethernet MAC standard includes historical half-duplex behavior as well as full-duplex operation, but the latter does not use collision detection. Saying “Ethernet always uses CSMA/CD” is therefore incorrect.

A switch separates the collision domains associated with older shared Ethernet designs. Frames can still be dropped because of congestion, errors or other conditions; those are not equivalent to half-duplex shared-medium collisions.

CSMA/CA: Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance

Wi-Fi stations commonly coordinate access to a shared radio channel using collision-avoidance rules. A station listens, waits according to interframe and contention rules, and uses a randomized backoff before attempting transmission. The goal is to reduce conflicting transmissions, not guarantee that two transmissions can never interfere.

A Wi-Fi transmitter cannot, in general, reliably listen for a collision while it is sending on the same channel in the way classic Ethernet collision detection was conceived. The receiver can instead acknowledge successfully received frames, and the sender can retry when the expected acknowledgment is missing.

Why do collisions still happen with CSMA/CA?

Two devices can choose overlapping transmission times. Wireless obstacles or distance may prevent them from hearing each other. Interference and changing radio conditions can also lead to retries. Carrier sensing is one input to contention management, but it is not perfect awareness of every transmission at every receiver.

What is the hidden-node problem?

Consider stations A and C that can both communicate with access point B but cannot hear one another directly. A may treat the channel as idle while C is already transmitting to B. Their signals can collide or interfere at B. This is a hidden-node scenario.

Optional request-to-send/clear-to-send (RTS/CTS) procedures can reduce some hidden-node contention by reserving transmission opportunities, but they add overhead and do not eliminate all interference or failures. Whether they are used depends on configuration and wireless implementation.

Token passing: a station transmits when it has permission

In a token-passing system, a special control token circulates among participating stations. A station ordinarily transmits when it holds the token and follows the protocol's rules, then passes the token onward. That coordination differs from every station independently competing for an idle medium.

Token Ring, associated with IEEE 802.5, is a classic educational example. Some other specialized networks have also used token-based arrangements. Token passing is not the mechanism used by ordinary modern Wi-Fi or full-duplex switched Ethernet.

What if the token is lost or a station fails?

Real token systems need recovery procedures for missing, duplicated or invalid tokens and for station changes. The existence of a token does not automatically mean that the network cannot fail; network design and protocol maintenance still matter.

Worked example: a collision on a shared Ethernet cable

Assume two stations share a half-duplex Ethernet segment. At time t=0, A decides the segment is idle and begins transmitting. B is far enough away that A's signal has not yet reached B. B therefore also begins transmitting. Both transmissions overlap, and the shared Ethernet collision-handling procedure becomes relevant. Randomized retransmission delays help prevent the same stations from colliding repeatedly.

Replace the shared medium with two separate, correctly configured full-duplex switch ports. The stations no longer compete in the same Ethernet collision domain. A switch can forward or queue frames, and overload may still cause drops, but CSMA/CD is no longer required to arbitrate a shared cable.

Worked example: two Wi-Fi devices in one classroom

A laptop and a phone both want to send data to an access point. Each follows wireless channel-access rules, including contention waiting and backoff. If both attempts overlap at the receiver, an acknowledgment may be missing and a sender may try again. Other devices sharing the channel can also introduce delays. This explains why a strong signal does not guarantee a fixed Wi-Fi throughput for each station.

With a hidden node, one device may not hear another, even though both can reach the access point. This is a wireless medium problem, not an IP routing error or a problem caused by choosing the wrong LAN address.

Where do these methods fit in the OSI model?

Medium access rules are associated with the MAC sublayer of the OSI data link layer (Layer 2) and with technology-specific physical-layer behavior. They decide how frames gain access to their medium; they do not replace IP routing, application security or encryption.

For the distinction between Logical Link Control (LLC) and Medium Access Control (MAC), read our LLC versus MAC lesson.

Do these methods provide security?

No. CSMA/CD, CSMA/CA and token passing organize medium access; none by itself authenticates users or encrypts their traffic. Wi-Fi security relies on additional mechanisms such as appropriate authentication and encryption configurations. Wired LANs need access controls and segmentation where warranted.

Do not confuse collision avoidance with intrusion prevention. A Wi-Fi network may avoid some transmission conflicts and still expose traffic or services if configured insecurely.

How to answer this topic in an exam

  1. First identify whether the connection is shared or point to point.
  2. For classic shared, half-duplex Ethernet, explain sensing, collision detection and backoff.
  3. For Wi-Fi, explain sensing, randomized contention, acknowledgments and hidden nodes.
  4. For Token Ring, explain that stations obtain permission through a token.
  5. Always state that normal switched full-duplex Ethernet does not use CSMA/CD.

Check 1: two workstations connect to separate full-duplex switch ports. Are Ethernet collisions handled with CSMA/CD? No. Check 2: two Wi-Fi clients interfere at one access point. Can CSMA/CA eliminate every collision? No. Check 3: which historical system commonly uses a circulating token? Token Ring.
